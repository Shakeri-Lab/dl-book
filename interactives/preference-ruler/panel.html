Watch the mechanism: a preference ruler with no fixed zero

If every reward score increases by the same amount, does a response become more likely to win?

Follow responses A and C from the transitive example above. First read their score difference; then add the audit's common shift to both scores.

\( \predictionpart{P(A\succ C)}=\sigma\!\left(\class{pr-difference}{\predictionpart{r_A}-\predictionpart{r_C}}\right) \) \( =\sigma\!\left((\predictionpart{r_A}+\class{pr-cancel}{c})-(\predictionpart{r_C}+\class{pr-cancel}{c})\right) \)

Larger scores, same gap, same probability. The additions cancel: reward has no identifiable zero.

The calculation is readable without playback. Opening this panel loads the optional controls.

These are the existing transitive example's scores and common shift, not a new fit.

Scope and caveats

The viewport follows the scores while tick spacing stays fixed; it does not compress the gap. The Bradley–Terry probability models a comparison under one prompt and review process, not correctness or universal value. The constant may depend on the prompt but must be shared by its candidates. This invariance does not generally hold for unequal additions or rescaling. Cyclic judgments still need the separate audit above.

Check yourself. Instead of adding a constant, double both scores: 1 and −1 become 2 and −2. Does the preference probability change?

Yes. The gap doubles to 4 and the probability rises from 0.881 to 0.982. The model cannot see a common shift, but it does see a change of scale.

Transcript and keyboard help
  1. Predict whether a common reward increase changes a pairwise preference probability.
  2. The circle marks response A with score 1. The diamond marks response C with score minus 1.
  3. The bracket measures A's score minus C's score: a gap of 2.
  4. The sigmoid maps that gap to a modeled preference probability of approximately 0.881, the transitive figure's A-to-C edge.
  5. Predict again before the shift: both scores will receive the same addition. Nothing moves during this beat; the ring records the probability before any motion.
  6. One amount is added to both scores. After a still moment the pair slides as one, bracket and all, through half of the shift. The viewport follows with unchanged tick spacing, and the probability mark stays inside its ring.
  7. Halfway, the gap is still 2 and the probability is still 0.881. The second half of the shift follows in the same way.
  8. Adding 37 gives scores 38 and 36. Their gap is still 2 and their preference probability is unchanged: the two common additions cancel in the difference. Only relative scores are identified; a larger raw number is not a new measurement of goodness.
