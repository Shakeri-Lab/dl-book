Nothing here is trained: the two detectives are the chapter's hand-built edge experts, and the pooling is the fixed 2 × 2 maximum LeNet uses.

Scope and caveats

The square, the vertical Sobel kernel and its transpose, and the two absolute-value reports are the chapter's corner-detector cell, which stacks them as two channels. That cell reads the stack at full resolution; this panel pools each report first, as LeNet does after every convolution, so the pooled values are computed here from the declared square and kernel and are not printed in the chapter.

Pooling takes one maximum per map and per 2 × 2 block. It shrinks the height and width by half, leaves the number of channels alone, and never compares one detective's number with another's. The counterfactual line, one maximum across both maps, is drawn only to show what that would cost: the square's side and its top would both read 4, and nothing downstream could tell them apart. What pooling does lose is position inside each block, which the chapter's pooling section and its replay measure separately.

Blue is the input image and green the detectives' reports, pooled or not; the windows are neutral ink because nothing in them is learned here. In a trained network the detectives are learned kernels and there are six of them in LeNet's first layer, not two.

Check yourself. LeNet's first layer hands its pool six maps of 28 × 28. What shape leaves the pool, and how deep is each of the second layer's kernels?

6 × 14 × 14, and each second-layer kernel is 6 × 5 × 5. Pooling halves the height and width of every map and leaves all six detectives in place; the next convolution is the step that reads the six reports together, which is why its kernels are six deep.

Transcript and keyboard help
  1. Predict. A white square on a black 28 by 28 image, and two empty maps: one for a vertical-edge detective, one for a horizontal-edge detective. After pooling, will the network still be able to tell the square's side from its top?
  2. Two detectives, one window. A three by three window sweeps the image once, and at every position both detectives write a reading into their own map. The vertical map lights the square's left and right sides; the horizontal map lights its top and bottom.
  3. One spot, two readings. At a point on the square's left side the pair reads 4 and 0; on its top, 0 and 4; at its corner, 3 and 3. The stack's depth says which clues are present at a spot.
  4. Pool each map. A two by two window runs through both maps in step, and each map keeps the largest of its own four numbers. Both maps shrink from 28 by 28 to 14 by 14; there are still two of them.
  5. Hold. The probe sits on the pooled block at the square's side and on the pooled block at its top. Predict: can the pooled pair still tell them apart?
  6. Yes. The side block reads 4 and 0 and the top block 0 and 4, because each map was pooled on its own. Had one maximum been taken across both maps, both blocks would read 4 and the difference would be gone.
  7. The shapes. The convolution took one channel of 28 by 28 to two channels of 28 by 28; pooling took two channels of 28 by 28 to two channels of 14 by 14. Convolution changed the depth, pooling the space.
