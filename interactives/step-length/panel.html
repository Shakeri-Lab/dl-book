Scope and caveats

This is full-batch gradient descent on one parameter, not the stochastic version this chapter is about: no minibatch, no noise, no epoch, and no model is fitted. The three rates, the start and the rule of thumb are the chapter's; the bowl is this panel's.

The bowl is declared, not measured: loss (w − 2.5)², curvature 2 — the curvature a standardized feature gives a squared-error loss, which is why the chapter's advice is about normalized toy problems. Its threshold, 2 ÷ 2 = 1, is computed in the browser from the declared curvature; a differently curved bowl has a different one, and the fact that this one lands on 1 is that curvature's coincidence, not a law. The minimum sits at the chapter's generating weight 2.5 and the ball starts at its printed w = −0.5.

Eight steps per rate is this panel's budget; the chapter's own helper runs sixty. The drawn window is w from −4 to 9: at α = 1.1 the ball leaves it after five steps, and from there the picture pins the ball to the frame while the readout keeps the true loss. Landings outside the window are not drawn.

The chapter's figure plots loss against step index on a log axis; this panel deliberately shows the geometry instead, so nothing here is that figure's curve and no step index is an axis. The figure's per-rate colours are not reused: on this picture wine is loss and orange is the parameter.

Decay schedules and warmup are named in the chapter's own rule-of-thumb callout and are out of scope here: α is constant inside every run. The threshold governs this full-batch quadratic only; with minibatch noise a rate well below it can still fail to settle, which is the region of confusion the previous section draws.

Check yourself. Leave the feature unnormalized so the bowl is ten times as curved, c = 20 instead of 2. Which of the chapter's three rates still converges?

Only 0.005. The threshold falls to 2 ÷ 20 = 0.1, so the chapter's middle rate is now past it: its factor is 1 − 0.12 × 20 = −1.4, and the distance to the bottom grows by 1.4 every step. That is what the words normalized toy problems are doing in the rule of thumb.

Transcript and keyboard help
  1. One slope. A ball rests at w = −0.5 on a quadratic bowl whose minimum is at 2.5. The loss there is 9.0000 and the slope is −6.0000, so the dashed arrow spans six units of w — which, on a bowl of curvature 2, is exactly the distance to the point of equal height on the far side.
  2. The first rate, 0.005. The solid arrow is that fraction of the dashed one: 0.0300 of a unit, a hair. Eight steps carry the ball from −0.5 only to −0.2682, and the loss from 9.0000 to 7.6631. Reaching the bottom at this rate takes 568 steps where 0.12 takes 21.
  3. The second rate, 0.12. The same rule and the same eight steps end at w = 2.1661, loss 0.1115. Each step multiplies the distance to the bottom by 0.76.
  4. The third rate, 1.1. The solid arrow is now longer than the dashed one and its tip, at 6.1000, is past the minimum and up the far wall. Predict: does this rate arrive late, or not at all?
  5. The run. Landings at 6.1000, −1.8200, 7.6840 and −3.7208 carry losses 12.9600, 18.6624, 26.8739 and 38.6984 — every one higher than the 9.0000 it started from, and each 1.44 times the one before. The fifth step leaves the picture; after eight the loss is 166.3958.
  6. The wall. The threshold is 2 divided by the curvature, here 2 ÷ 2 = 1. At exactly that rate the step lands on the ring at 5.5, the point of equal height, and the bounce repeats for ever at loss 9.0000.
  7. A tenth under the wall, 0.9. The ball still crosses the valley every step, but each landing is lower: 4.9000, 0.5800, 4.0360 and on down to 1.9967, loss 0.2533.
  8. Back below the wall. The rate glides to 0.12 and the ball rests at the bottom again. The chapter's rule of thumb starts near 0.1, a tenth of this bowl's wall; read the curves, raise a crawling rate and lower an exploding one.
