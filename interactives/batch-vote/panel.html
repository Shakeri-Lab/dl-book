Scope and caveats

Both laws are the chapter's; the toy problem is a declared computed variant, so the sixty-four gradients, their average, σ and every jitter reading are computed from the panel's own attributes rather than printed anywhere in the book. The jitter readout and both curves are the exact standard deviation over all uniform subsets of that size; the sixty-four diamonds are sixty-four actual batches from a declared deterministic family — one declared shuffle of the examples, then its sixty-four cyclic windows of length B — so every example sits in exactly B of them and their centre is the full-batch gradient at every B; at B = 1 they are exactly the sixty-four per-example gradients, at B = n they are all the whole population, and in between their spread is close to, not identical with, the curve. This is the chapter's Case 1: the loss is a mean of per-example terms, so a batch average is an unbiased estimate of the full gradient and B is a compute-and-noise dial, not a correctness dial. A batch does not estimate a nonlinear functional of an aggregate without bias, and where the batch defines the objective it is protocol, not an estimator; the chapter separates those cases. Unbiasedness holds at a fixed parameter vector, which is what this scene shows — it is not a claim about successive reshuffled batches along a training run. Cost here is the count of per-example gradients one step needs, not a measured time; larger B costs proportionally more of them, which is the trade the chapter names. The batch size is the scene's one control; dragging it pauses playback, and any timeline action restores the timeline's own value.

Check yourself. A training set of one million examples, batch raised from 100 to 400. By what factor does the gradient's standard deviation change, and how much does the finite-population factor contribute?

Almost exactly one half. One over the square root of four is 0.5; the correction moves only from 0.99995 at B equal to 100 to 0.99980 at 400, so the ratio is 0.49992. For B far below n the correction is near one — the chapter's simpler rule.

Transcript and keyboard help
  1. Sixty-four per-example gradients sit as grey squares in the gradient plane. Their average, the full-batch gradient (7.50, 3.75), is the bold arrow with a ring at its tip.
  2. The jitter scale opens beside the plane. At a batch of one the estimates are the examples themselves, and the standard deviation of the estimate is 11.21.
  3. Held at a batch of one. Quadrupling to four costs four times as much; predict how much of that jitter it removes before releasing.
  4. Released. The batch grows one example at a time to four, and the cloud of sixty-four estimates pulls in toward the true gradient.
  5. At four the jitter is 5.47: the ratio is 0.49, one halving, not the fall to a quarter that proportional thinking expects. Both law curves are drawn.
  6. The batch quadruples again, to sixteen, and the marker slides down the curve toward its flat right-hand end.
  7. At sixteen the jitter is 2.45, a ratio of 0.45. The curve ahead is almost flat: the remaining forty-eight examples have little left to buy.
  8. The batch reaches sixty-four, the whole population. Every one of the sixty-four estimates is the true gradient, the jitter is exactly 0.00, and the dashed independent-sampling law still reads 1.40.
