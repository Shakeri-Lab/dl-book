Scope and caveats

The point and turn specify drawing geometry, not measured values. Both maps change together: the encoder gives \(\matr{Q}^{\top}\vect{z}\), and the decoder uses \(\matr{V}_k\matr{Q}\). Changing only one map need not preserve the reconstruction. The later curve experiment has a one-dimensional code; its orthogonal basis change is a sign flip, not this two-axis rotation.

Check yourself. Let Q flip the sign of the second basis vector instead of turning the basis. Does the reconstruction move?

No. A reflection is orthogonal too: the second coordinate changes sign, its basis vector changes sign, and their product is unchanged. The reconstruction depends on the subspace, not on how its axes are labelled.

Transcript and keyboard help
  1. A green point marks the reconstruction inside a fixed two-dimensional subspace. Predict whether turning its coordinate axes must move this point.
  2. The two solid arrows are the columns of an orthonormal basis. Their directions name coordinates, not a different reconstruction plane.
  3. Perpendicular drops read the point's coordinates on the current basis axes. The axes then begin to turn within the same plane.
  4. The new basis is V times Q. Dashed axes retain the original reference. The new encoder coordinates are Q transpose times the old coordinates.
  5. The turn finishes. The coordinate projections differ, but the green reconstruction point has not moved.
  6. Decode by adding the new coordinate-weighted basis vectors head to tail. The two components still end at the same green point.
  7. The paired basis changes cancel: Q times Q transpose is the identity. The reconstruction projector is unchanged.
  8. Compare projectors rather than raw basis columns or latent coordinates. This schematic coordinate change is not optimizer motion or the later one-dimensional experiment.
