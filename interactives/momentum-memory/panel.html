Watch the mechanism: how one rule damps a zigzag and speeds a crawl

Momentum gives the update a memory, at the same step length. Does that speed the iterate up in every direction?

The chapter's own velocity recursion and its β ∈ [0.9, 0.99], run at β = 0.9 on the narrow quadratic valley this panel declares: two runs from one start, one step length, and the velocity written out term by term in each direction.

Momentum is not a larger learning rate: both runs here use the same α = 0.05, and a quadratic valley is the easy case that real loss surfaces are not.

Scope and caveats

The valley is schematic: a declared separable quadratic with curvature 40 across and 1 along, started at (0.25, 10) so that both components of the first gradient are exactly 10, and run for eight steps at α = 0.05. The chapter's own ill-conditioned race is seeded and its iterates are never printed, so none of those numbers could be mirrored here. Every gradient, velocity, link and position on this picture is computed in the browser from the declared fixture. The recursion, the range of β and the cancel-or-accumulate behaviour are the chapter's.

β = 0.9 is the low end of the chapter's printed range, and the value its own momentum=0.9 race uses. At that setting the across gradient reverses about every second step rather than literally every step; plain SGD's reverses every single step. The arithmetic is the same either way: a term that disagrees with the accumulated sum subtracts from it.

Plain SGD is the same recursion with nothing kept — β = 0 — so its velocity is the bare gradient, which is what its rail draws; during that sweep the rail reads the gradient at the drawn position, exact at every step and interpolated between. With α = 0.05 and curvature 40, αλ = 2 exactly, so plain SGD's across component sits on the oscillation boundary and neither grows nor dies. A smaller α would damp that zigzag and slow the crawl further, which is the one-dial problem momentum is for.

Drawing devices: the wide layout draws both parameter axes at one scale, 50 pixels per unit, and the narrow layout shows the same window in a taller box, magnifying the across axis about twice; the two gradient legs at the iterate share one declared magnification of 2.2 pixels per gradient unit, so two equal components draw as two equal legs; the two rails share one scale, and the band on each marks ten, the size of the first gradient in both directions. The velocity points uphill, as the recursion writes it, and the step taken is α times it, backwards.

Momentum's along component keeps its sign at every one of the eight steps, and the eighth step carries the iterate 0.6754 past the bottom: speed builds, and a ninth step's gradient would finally point the other way. Nothing is trained, no loss is plotted, and the step counts are not a claim that one optimizer wins at a shared learning rate — the chapter's own race figure is that comparison, and this panel is not it. Nesterov momentum and Adam are named in the chapter and are out of scope here.

Check yourself. Raise β from 0.9 to 0.95 and hold α. One direction's gradient stays at 10 every step; another alternates between +10 and −10. What does each direction's velocity settle to?

The agreeing direction settles at 10/(1−β): 100 at β = 0.9, 200 at β = 0.95 — doubled. The alternating one settles at ±10/(1+β): ±5.2632, then ±5.1282 — slightly smaller. Raising β widens the gap from 19 times to 39 times. More memory does not mean bigger steps everywhere; it sharpens the selection.

Transcript and keyboard help
  1. A narrow valley, drawn as nested level curves of the declared quadratic: steep across, shallow along, with the bottom at the right. One iterate stands at (0.25, 10), and the gradient there splits into two legs of exactly the same length, because both its components are 10.
  2. Plain SGD takes eight steps at α = 0.05. The iterate zigzags across the steep direction — the across gradient flips sign every single step — and inches along the shallow one, ending 6.6342 from the bottom. Its rail carries one link, the current gradient, because plain SGD keeps nothing.
  3. Back to the same start with the same step length, and this time a velocity that remembers, at β = 0.9. Both rails are empty and share one scale; the shaded band on each marks 10, the size of that first gradient. Does the memory speed up both directions?
  4. Two steps, each performed in the order the recursion is written: the running sum shrinks by β, this step's gradient is added to its tip, then the iterate moves. Step one puts 10 on both rails. Step two adds −10 across, which wipes the shrunken 9 down to −1, and +9.5 along, which lifts it to 18.5.
  5. Steps three and four. Two agreeing contributions build the across sum out to −8.9; the reversal at step four wipes it back to 1.79. The along sum, with nothing disagreeing, passes three times one contribution at 30.0162.
  6. Steps five and six. The along sum peaks at 33.7164, more than three contributions, and then eases because its own gradient is dying as the bottom approaches. The across sum keeps folding through zero.
  7. Steps seven and eight. Eight terms on each rail: the across chain has folded back and forth and ends at 2.8355, never once having outgrown the first single contribution of 10; the along chain has marched out to 30.3916.
  8. Both paths together, eight steps each at the same α. Plain SGD is still 6.6342 from the bottom; momentum has arrived and run 0.6754 past it. One rule, one step length, opposite outcomes in the two directions.
