Watch the mechanism: every round counts the pairs again

Byte pair encoding merges the most frequent adjacent pair, then repeats. At its first count, w+e has 8, across newest and lower, and l+o has 7, across low and lower. Its first three merges each use 9 weighted occurrences. Which pair is merged fourth?

The loop below starts every round with counts: Counter[tuple[str, str]] = Counter(), a fresh count of the current pieces. Here are its four counted words as tiles.

\( \text{count}(a{+}b)=\sum_{\text{words}}\text{frequency}\times\#\bigl(a\ b\ \text{adjacent}\bigr),\quad \class{pr-recount}{\text{taken on the current pieces every round}} \)

Counts are redone each round: e+s took newest's e, so w+e fell to 2 and l+o won.

Scope and caveats

Every count is computed from the corpus line by the loop below. The chapter's text names only the nine and the seven; the 8, 6 and 2 of w+e are this panel's recount of the same loop. The loop counts fourteen distinct adjacent pairs in round 1. The picture draws two of them and the maximum, and it folds the counts of rounds 3 and 5 into their merges.

The lesson does not rest on the tie-break. With the reverse tie order, w+e keeps 8 through the three nines and drops to 2 after the third merge, and the final pieces are the same. The merge names do change (t+</w>, s+t</w>, e+st</w>, o+w, l+ow): the fourth merge is then o+w, the other 7, not l+o, and w+e still never merges. </w> sorts before letters in lexicographic order. It decides no tie here: every tie in these five rounds is settled by the first piece. With lower counted 3, w+e would tie the three nines and lose to e+s in lexicographic order. The check below takes 4.

An arc marks one adjacent occurrence of a drawn pair, and a bar adds up those occurrences, each weighted by its word's frequency. The dashed outline keeps w+e's first count for comparison; the loop keeps no such record. Where lower's w+e stood, the pair after merge 5 is low+e, counted 2. The loop stops after five merges, a chosen budget, not at any convergence, so no count follows the last merge; the live w+e bar is retired once its last arc is gone.

BERT's own tokenizer scores merges differently, and practical byte-level tokenizers begin from bytes. A finished merge list is applied in its own order when new text is split. The recount shown here is how that order is found.

Check yourself. Suppose lower were counted 4 times instead of 2, all else unchanged. Which pair merges first, and what does e+s count in the round after?

w+e: 6 + 4 = 10 beats every 9, and five pairs now tie at 9, since l+o and o+w rise to 5 + 4. The merge fuses w and e in newest and lower, so the next round counts e+s at only the 3 from widest. A merge removes another pair only where the two share a symbol occurrence.
