The calculation is readable without playback. Opening this panel loads the optional controls.

The values 0.80 and 0.30 are an illustrative toy: a real cell gives every coordinate its own keep, computed by a sigmoid from the input and the old state.

Scope and caveats

The two values are round numbers inside the range of tanh, which shapes every candidate; the chapter trains its gates and prints no state values. A real state is a vector, and every coordinate of \( \vect{z}_t \) is a keep of its own, so one coordinate can hold its value while its neighbor is overwritten.

The reset gate decides how much of the old state the candidate reads: it shapes the candidate before the blend and is not drawn. PyTorch’s reset-after candidate changes how the candidate is computed, not this blend.

This panel follows the chapter’s convention: \( \vect{z}_t \) weights the old state, so it reads as keep. Some references swap the roles; compare the interpolation equation, not the gate’s name.

Blue marks every value the cell carries: the filled circle is the old state, the hollow diamond the candidate, the ring the new state, and the arrows their kept and written shares, filled and hollow like the marks they come from. The keep and write bar and the dial are neutral ink: a gate weighs values and carries none. The bar puts write on the left and keep on the right, the sides where the candidate and the old state sit on the line and where the dial writes all or keeps all, and each share is named beside its value's mark.

Check yourself. A GRU starts from h0 = 0, and every candidate it writes is a tanh output inside (−1, 1). Can a coordinate of its state ever reach 1.2, whatever the gates do?

No. Each step lands the new state on the segment between the old state and the candidate, since keep and write are shares of one whole. If both ends lie inside (−1, 1), so does the new state, and from h0 = 0 every state stays inside (−1, 1).

Transcript and keyboard help
  1. A number line from −1 to 1, the range of tanh. The old state sits at 0.80, a filled circle; the candidate at 0.30, a hollow diamond. The keep gate is fully open, so the bar is all keep and the new state, a ring, sits under the old state at 0.80.
  2. The keep halves to 0.5: the bar splits into write on the left and keep on the right, half each, and the new state leaves the picture. Predict: where does it land?
  3. The old state becomes an arrow from 0 to 0.80, and the keep shrinks it to half: 0.5 × 0.80 = 0.40.
  4. The candidate becomes an arrow from 0 to 0.30. What is not kept is written: it shrinks to 0.5 × 0.30 = 0.15 and slides onto the tip of the kept arrow, and the new state lands at that tip, 0.40 + 0.15 = 0.55.
  5. The segment from the candidate to the old state is marked. With equal shares, 0.55 sits halfway along it.
  6. The dial turns up to full keep. The kept arrow grows back to 0.80, the written arrow shrinks to nothing, and the new state slides along the segment to 0.80.
  7. The dial turns down to keep 0. The kept arrow shrinks to nothing and the written arrow carries the whole candidate: the new state slides the length of the segment to 0.30, overwritten.
  8. Home at keep 0.5: 0.40 kept plus 0.15 written gives 0.55. One dial does two jobs, keeping z of the old state and writing 1 − z of the candidate, so the new state always lands on the segment between them. Drag the dial to try another keep.
