Watch the mechanism: where "nearest neighbour" stops meaning anything

Covering 784 dimensions densely would take 9784 examples, so that route is closed. Then use the examples you have and trust the nearest ones — how near is the nearest, next to the farthest?

Not the chapter's measured curve but the geometry under it: the whole band of distances between two random points in a cube, drawn against the typical distance, as the dimension grows.

Width falls as one over the square root of d. Data cannot buy it back.

This is the unconstrained cube, not natural images: the chapter says so plainly, and structured data may concentrate near a much lower-dimensional set where near neighbours still exist.

Scope and caveats

The band is a closed-form estimate, not a simulation. For two points drawn uniformly from the unit cube the squared distance has mean d/6 and variance 7d/180, so the distance itself has a standard deviation that tends to a constant while its mean grows like the square root of d: the relative spread is the square root of 0.35 divided by d. The band's edges are placed at the expected extreme of a sample that size, which for 300 points is about 2.7131 standard deviations either way. Nothing here is sampled and no seed is used, so the panel's numbers are not the chapter's run — they are what its run should find, and at 784 dimensions they agree to the printed digit.

The ladder starts at ten dimensions because the estimate is a large-d one: at the chapter's smallest dimension, two, the spread is so large that the band would reach below zero, which is a sign the normal approximation has stopped applying rather than a claim about distance. The chapter's own measurement covers that case.

The ratio drawn here is the ratio of the extremes of the whole band, which is the same quantity the chapter averages over points, not a per-point average; the two agree at 784 to the digit the chapter prints. Grey is the band and the scenery, and the book's ink marks the two extremes and the ratio they make.

Check yourself. A dataset lives in 784 dimensions but every example actually lies on a smooth 12-dimensional surface inside it. Which number governs whether near neighbours still mean something — and by roughly how much does the band widen?

The intrinsic 12, not the ambient 784. The spread goes as one over the square root of the dimension that the data actually varies in, so the band is about eight times wider than the ambient number suggests — the square root of 784 over 12. That is why representation learning is worth so much: it is the dimension you cannot see that sets the price.
