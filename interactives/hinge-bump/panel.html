Watch the mechanism: three ramps that never come back down

Three ramps that never come back down — how can their sum return to zero?

These three hinges are this chapter's hinge-bump-values cell, plotted just above. The panel adds them one at a time onto the finished bump. Its peak and its four slopes are computed from the cell; the chapter prints neither.

\( \displaystyle \predictionpart{g}(\featurepart{x}) = h_1 \: \class{hb-coef}{-2} h_2 + h_3 \)

Three endless ramps, one local bump: nothing outside the window.

The calculation is readable without playback. Opening this panel loads the optional controls.

Nothing here is learned: the three breakpoints and the coefficients 1, −2, 1 are placed by hand, as the chapter places them, which is why nothing on the picture is drawn in the colour reserved for a learnable parameter, and this panel has no control to turn. The pale green shape standing from the first frame is the declared bump drawn ahead of its own construction — a target to aim at, not a second measurement — which is why it is the same green as the sum that lands on it, and why it is consumed the moment the sum reaches it. Each ramp's entry amount, 0 to 1, is a drawing device of the timeline rather than a quantity of the chapter: during the fold one eased amount both swings the middle ramp down to −2 h₂ and admits it into the sum, so no intermediate coefficient is ever written. The finished bump has four straight pieces, of slopes 0, +1, −1, 0, of which the panel writes at most two at a time; the rest are in the transcript. With the coefficient +1 instead of −2 the sum would keep climbing, and −2 is what turns it over — that is a remark about how the chapter's combination is built, not something this panel shows. One bump is a construction, not an approximation theorem: how many hinges a given target needs, and whether training finds them, are the chapter's next questions and not this panel's claim.

Beside them, pale green, the finished bump already stands as a target, labelled bump. Before playing on: how could a sum of three such ramps ever return to zero?
  2. The running sum draws on in green as the first ramp alone: flat, then climbing. Its slope on that climbing piece, +1, is written against the piece.
  3. One eased move swings the dashed ramp down from its breakpoint into −2 h₂ and folds the sum with it. The name −2 h₂ and the second slope, −1, arrive once the swing has finished, and the identity g(x) = h₁ − 2 h₂ + h₃ appears with the −2 washed.
  4. The dotted ramp joins: past 2.5 its slope +1 cancels the −1, the sum flattens onto the target, and the target is consumed, having been reached. The +1 and −1 retire; the slope of the flat piece, 0, is written; the peak height 2 is written at the apex, which is g(0.5) = 2; and a bracket under the ticks marks [−1.5, 2.5]. The four pieces, in order, have slopes 0, +1, −1, 0.
  5. The three ramps fade to a faint ghost. Three ramps that climb for ever, one bump that is zero everywhere outside one window.
