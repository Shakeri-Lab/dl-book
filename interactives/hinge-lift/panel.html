Scope and caveats

The XOR rows, the ReLU, the neuron and its weights are the chapter's; the rest is declared on this panel and solved from it. The swept fan of lines illustrates the chapter's "a hard linear boundary can classify at most three of the four points"; a fan of placements is not a proof, and the proof is the chapter's, not this picture's. The third axis is one neuron's activation — the same quantity the figure above contours — not a learned embedding dimension, and it is drawn at its own vertical scale, 0.7 drawing units per unit of activation, so heights are read from its ticks rather than against the two input axes. The cut is solved, not fitted: it is the unique plane whose four vertical clearances are equal, and they are equal because those four clearances always sum to the same 0.25 the rectifier clipped off at the origin. That cut uses all three coordinates, x1, x2 and h — the flat start of its tilt, a horizontal plane, is what a rule that kept only h would be, and it gets two of four; one neuron alone does not do XOR, and the chapter's next section builds boundaries out of several hinges. Inside the drawn window the final boundary reads as two separate straight pieces; they are two arms of one bent line whose corner lies well outside the frame, at x = (−2.07, 3.57). The drawn window, the swept fan, the flat starting plane and the camera are drawing devices; the four corners, their heights and every intersection are exact.

Check yourself. Keep w = (1.0, 0.65) but set b = 0, so every corner is ON. Could a flat cut still separate the lifted points?

No. With b = 0 the stimuli are 0, 0.65, 1.00 and 1.65 and nothing is clipped, so the heights are just a linear function of the inputs and all four lifted points lie on one plane: 0 + 1.65 = 0.65 + 1.00. The fold, not the third axis, is what separates them.

Transcript and keyboard help
  1. Four XOR corners sit on a schematic input plane, seen from one fixed angle: hollow rings at (0,0) and (1,1) are the 0s, filled squares at (0,1) and (1,0) are the 1s. The chapter's own straight rule, x₁ + x₂ = 0.5, is drawn across them; the corner it gets wrong wears a wine ring and the readout says 3 of 4.
  2. The line turns. The corner it gets wrong changes, and partway round two corners are wrong at once, so the readout falls to 2 of 4 and climbs back to 3. It never reads 4. The turn comes to rest with the line lying exactly along this neuron's own threshold, x₁ + 0.65x₂ = 0.25.
  3. That resting line is now the neuron's, and it turns orange. One side is OFF, where the neuron is silent, and it is shaded; the other is ON. A weight arrow grows out of the line, pointing straight across it.
  4. A third axis rises. The ON half of the plane tilts up into a sheet and each corner climbs to its own activation: 0.00, 0.40, 0.75 and 1.40. The OFF corner at (0,0) stays pinned flat at zero, because the rectifier clipped it.
  5. A hollow ghost drops out of that pinned corner to −0.25, the stimulus the rectifier threw away. Without the clip the four heights would be −0.25, 0.40, 0.75 and 1.40, and every one of them would lie on a single plane.
  6. A flat plane arrives horizontal — a rule that reads the activation alone — and gets 2 of 4. It tilts, keeping perfectly flat, bringing the two input coordinates back in. At 3 of 4 the (1,0) square passes below it; at 4 of 4 the (0,0) ring passes above it, and the tilt stops.
  7. Where that flat cut crosses the tilted sheet, a line is drawn in the air, and it falls to the input plane beneath it. A second piece of the boundary was already lying flat inside the OFF region.
  8. The input plane now carries a two-piece boundary with the two 0 corners shaded off and the two 1 corners left in the band between them. The cut is still perfectly flat; the sheet under it is what bends.
