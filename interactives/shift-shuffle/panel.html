Watch the mechanism: why scrambling is free and sliding is not

Scrambling every pixel position cost the retrained model nothing; sliding the image two pixels nearly halved it. Both rearrange the same pixels — why is only one of them free?

Scope and caveats

The permutation is free only because it is applied to the weights as well — which is what retraining on a scrambled world does. The chapter's shuffle experiment retrains, and the model it compares against was fitted on the scrambled pixels; a permutation applied to the pixels of an already fitted model would break the pairing exactly as a shift does. That is the whole asymmetry, and it is a statement about the hypothesis class, not about any particular fitted weight.

The slide follows the chapter's own shift_right: pixels move two places right, zeros enter at the left, and what runs off the right is lost. In a real image the slide is two-dimensional and a row of 784 wraps as 28 rows of 28, so a two-pixel horizontal shift moves each pixel two positions within its row rather than two positions along the flat vector; the one-dimensional row here keeps the re-pairing visible and drops that detail.

Nothing here explains how much accuracy either change costs. The chapter measures that, and the loss from a shift depends on what the fitted weights were doing, which no schematic row can say. Blue is the pixel input, orange the learnable weights, green the score they produce, and grey the reference the comparison beat keeps.

Check yourself. Take the fitted model and permute only the validation pixels, leaving the weights as they were. Would that cost accuracy — and does a convolutional layer share the dense layer's indifference to a common permutation?

Yes, and no. Permuting the pixels alone re-pairs every term exactly as a shift does, so the score changes and the accuracy collapses; the shuffle experiment is free only because it retrains. A convolution is not permutation-indifferent at all: its weights are tied to relative positions, so scrambling the grid destroys the adjacency it is built to exploit.

Transcript and keyboard help
  1. Predict. Eight pixel values, 0.20, 0.90, 0.10, 0.60, 0.80, 0.30, 0.70 and 0.40, sit above the eight weights 0.90, minus 0.40, 0.20, 0.70, minus 0.60, 0.30, 0.80 and minus 0.10. No products are drawn yet.
  2. The sum. Each weight multiplies the pixel above it: 0.18, minus 0.36, 0.02, 0.42, minus 0.48, 0.09, 0.56 and minus 0.04. Their total, the unit's score, is 0.39.
  3. Hold. The pixel positions are about to be scrambled by one fixed permutation, and the model retrained, which scrambles the weights the same way. Predict the score before releasing it.
  4. Scrambled together. Every column slides to a new place carrying both its pixel and its weight, so every product travels without changing height. The same eight numbers are added in a different order, and the score is 0.39 again.
  5. Hold. The columns return to their original order, and now the pixels alone will slide two places to the right while the weights stay put. Predict again.
  6. Slid. The pixels move right by two, two zeros enter at the left, and the last two values fall off the end. Every weight now meets a different pixel, so the products are remade: 0.00, 0.00, 0.04, 0.63, minus 0.06, 0.18, 0.64 and minus 0.03. The score is 1.40.
  7. Compare. A grey mark holds the score the unit had in place, 0.39, against the 1.40 the same weights now produce. The bar is three and a half times as long.
  8. The lesson. A sum does not care what order its terms arrive in, which is why a permutation applied to both rows is free. It cares very much which weight met which pixel, which is why a slide is not.
