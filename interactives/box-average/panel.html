Scope and caveats

The magnified view above the window is a drawing device, not a second signal: it shows the nine samples the window currently covers at a legible size, with each one shrunk to a ninth and the nine pieces laid end to end, which is what multiplying by the weights and summing does. The samples are the output of the chapter's own cell, seeded with 6050, transcribed at seven decimals; every average is recomputed from them and agrees with the chapter's F.conv1d to better than one part in a million.

Each average is drawn at the centre of its window, which is the convention the chapter's figure uses when it pairs the averages with t[4:-4]. A window nine samples wide fits in 300 − 9 + 1 = 292 places, so the first four and the last four samples are never a centre. Padding the ends would give them averages, at the price of inventing samples outside the signal; the chapter does not pad, and neither does this picture.

Blue is the input signal and green the averages the filter produces. The window and its weights are drawn in neutral ink: they are fixed here, and the book keeps orange for weights that are learned, which begins in the next chapter.

Check yourself. Make the window 25 samples wide. How many averages come out, and what slice of t would the plotting line need?

276, and t[12:-12]. A window 25 samples wide fits in 300 − 25 + 1 = 276 places, and its centre can never sit within 12 samples of either end, so twelve samples at each end have no average of their own. The wider window smooths more and loses more.

Transcript and keyboard help
  1. Predict. Three hundred noisy samples lie in a row, and a window nine samples wide covers the first nine. Each average will be drawn at the centre of its window. Which samples will never get one?
  2. The weights. A magnified view opens above the window and shows its nine samples as stems. Every stem carries the same weight, one ninth.
  3. One average. Each stem shrinks to a ninth of its height, and the nine pieces are laid end to end. Where they end is the average, 0.17, and it lands at the window's centre, the fifth sample.
  4. Slide. The window moves one sample to the right, covers a new nine, and stacks them again: 0.20, 0.24, 0.27, 0.35 and 0.39 follow, each at its own centre.
  5. The whole signal. The magnified view closes and the window sweeps to the end, repeating the same stack at every position and writing the smooth curve as it goes.
  6. Hold. The window has reached the last sample and can go no further. Which samples never got an average?
  7. The ends. The first four samples and the last four were never the centre of a window: 300 samples give 300 minus 9 plus 1, that is 292 averages, which is why the chapter plots them against t from index 4 to four before the end.
  8. Keep the slide, change the nine numbers. Every filter in the rest of this chapter makes exactly this move with a different set of weights.
